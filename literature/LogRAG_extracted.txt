Abstract—Log-based anomaly detection is critical in moni-
toring the operations of information systems and in the real-
time reporting of system failures. Utilizing deep learning-based
log anomaly detection methods facilitates effective detection of
anomalies within logs. However, existing methods are greatly
dependent on log parsers, and parsing errors can considerably
affect downstream anomaly detection tasks. Additionally, meth-
ods that predict the next log event in a sequence are susceptible
to the instability of sequences and the emergence of unseen logs
as systems evolve, resulting in a higher false positive rate. In this
paper, we put forward LogRAG, a semi-supervised log anomaly
detection framework based on retrieval-augmented generation
(RAG). This framework conducts phased detection using both
Log Tokens and Log Templates to mitigate the impact of log
parsing errors. It also utilizes a single-class classifier to model the
normal behavior of the system, thereby circumventing the effects
of unstable sequences. Finally, it employs large language model
(LLM) empowered by RAG to reevaluate detected anomalous
logs, thereby improving accuracy. LogRAG demonstrates a 15%
improvement in F1 Score on the BGL dataset and a 60%
improvement on the Spirit dataset when compared to the previous
best semi-supervised learning algorithm.
Index Terms—Log Analysis, Log Anomaly Detection, Deep
Learning, Large Language Model, Retrieval-Augmented Gener-
ation
I. INTRODUCTION
As networks and systems become more complex, the fre-
quency of faults and exceptions increases. These issues not
only compromise user experience but may also lead to system
crashes. With the growing intricacy of these systems, manual
fault detection becomes increasingly impractical. Log files,
crucial for recording the runtime states of these systems, are
essential in the automated detection and prediction of system
anomalies. Log files record the runtime states of these systems,
and the prevailing approach to detecting and predicting system
anomalies relies on automated log analysis. Therefore, logs are
currently widely used in automated monitoring methods for
anomaly detection [1]–[3], root cause analysis [4]–[6], failure
prediction [7], and other system automation processes [8]. In
this paper, our primary focus lies on log anomaly detection
methods utilizing deep learning. [9]

An anomalous log signifies the presence of an anomaly in
the network or system [10]. Log anomaly detection typically
involves converting unstructured log information into struc-
tured data for analysis, and the process commonly referred to
as log parsing or log template extraction [11]. After acquiring
structured log data, deep learning methods are used for system
modeling. Common approaches [1], [12]–[14] to log anomaly
detection typically include predicting the next log event in
a sequence and comparing it with the actual log events to
identify anomalies. Those methods that model the system’s
operational state based on log sequences and utilize them
for anomaly detection have achieved commendable results
on public datasets. However, in real-world scenarios, merely
identifying anomalies within a specific window at a coarse
granularity often proves insufficient. Furthermore, sequence
models employed for anomaly detection lack the capability for
flexible adjustment of detection granularity. Consequently, this
paper primarily concentrates on exploring anomaly detection
methods that do not rely on log sequences.
Nevertheless, existing methods face three main challenges.
(1)Log parsing errors. Current log anomaly detection methods
heavily depend on log parsers. However, log parsers may
not accurately parse keywords and parameters, leading to
the omission of crucial semantic information. These semantic
details could be crucial for determining whether a log is
anomalous or not. As shown in Fig.1, parsing errors can lead
to the loss of key information, such as interrupt, in the original
logs. This results in parsed data that is unable to distinguish be-
tween normal and anomalous conditions. Minor parsing errors
can significantly impact downstream anomaly detection tasks
[15]. (2) Sequence modeling difficulty. Unstable sequences

execution order of a program. As mentioned in EvLog [16],
these sequences can result from interleaved log messages
across different threads, the introduction of new log events
due to software updates, or system evolution. Existing methods
often rely on sequence modeling, but these approaches may
struggle to address the instability induced by these factors. On
the other hand, sequence modeling methods must train models
using log sequences of specified window sizes, thus precluding
flexible adjustment of the granularity of the anomaly detection

the training phase. Numerous studies have investigated the

10.1% of log events remained unchanged, while over 40% of
the 1532 unique log events were classified as dissimilar under
its experimental conditions. Existing methods often struggle to
adapt to these unseen logs, leading to the misclassification of
numerous unseen normal logs as anomalies and consequently
generating a significant number of false positives.
To address the above challenges, we present LogRAG, a
semi-supervised log anomaly detection framework that utilizes
RAG for post-processing. Similar to other semi-supervised
learning methods, LogRAG only requires training with logs
from the system’s normal operation state, tolerating a small
amount of anomalous logs without the need for manually
labeled data. By utilizing Log Tokens (In Section III-B2, this
will be explained in detail.), LogRAG mitigates the risk of
log parsing errors, while the deep single-class classifier learns
the system’s normal behavior without relying on sequence
information, thereby mitigating the impact of unstable log
sequences. Subsequently, for logs detected as anomalies, we
design a post-processing stage based on RAG for reevaluation.
Through retrieving abnormal log templates from historical
normal data, constructing prompts, and utilizing LLM for
anomaly detection, LogRAG significantly decreases false pos-
itives.
Experiments were conducted on two widely used datasets
to validate LogRAG’s effectiveness. LogRAG outperformed
nearly all semi-supervised methods and even exceeded su-
pervised methods in certain scenarios. Additionally, we in-
vestigated the improvement in precision for online anomaly
detection scenarios using RAG with LogRAG. Furthermore,
we assessed the contributions of different components within
LogRAG.
Our contributions can be summarized as follows:
• We utilize Log Tokens and Log Templates separately in
the anomaly detection and post-processing stages, thus
reducing the computational overhead of Log Templates
parsing and mitigating the impact of parsing errors.
• We employ a deep single-class classifier to model the
normal operation of the system without the need for
log sequence modeling, thereby enhancing adaptability
to changes in system states.
• By incorporating LLM and designing retrieval strategies
and prompts, we introduce the first application of RAG in
the post-processing stage of log anomaly detection. This
significantly reduces false positives generated by semi-
supervised anomaly detection, thereby reducing the cost
of manual judgment.

II. RELATED WORK
A. Deep learning-based anomaly detection
In recent years, log anomaly detection methods based on
deep learning have been proposed with the aim of automat-
ically detecting defects and faults present in system logs.
Supervised methods train models to discern whether unknown
logs are normal or abnormal, utilizing a large amount of fully
labeled log data. NeuralLog [15] detects anomalies in log
data by directly converting raw log messages into semantic
vectors and using a Transformer-based model, bypassing the
need for log parsing and improving accuracy with OOV
(out-of-vocabulary) words. LogRobust [14] addresses log data
instability by transforming log events into semantic vectors
and employing an attention-based Bi-LSTM model to robustly
detect anomalies in the presence of evolving log statements
and processing noise. SwissLog [18] uses a dictionary-based
parser and BERT to create semantic and temporal embeddings,
which are then analyzed by an Attention-based Bi-LSTM
model to detect anomalies in system logs. Lu et al. [19]
present a CNN-based approach for anomaly detection in big
data system logs. However, in practice, obtaining labeled logs
is challenging, and as the system evolves, the labels for the
logs may also change.
In contrast, semi-supervised learning methods typically only
require logs from the system operating under normal condi-
tions. These methods train a model that represents normal
operation, and logs that deviate from this normal model are
considered anomalies. DeepLog [1] employs a deep neural
network model with LSTM to treat system logs as natural
language sequences. This allows the model to learn normal
log patterns and detect anomalies when deviations from these
patterns occur. LogAnomaly [12] introduces a framework
that models log streams as natural language sequences and
employs template2vec, a novel method to extract semantic
information from log templates. It is capable of detecting
both sequential and quantitative anomalies simultaneously,
avoiding false alarms caused by new log templates appearing
between periodic model retraining. PLELog [13] is a semi-
supervised approach that only requires labels for a subset of
normal log sequences. It utilizes probabilistic label estimation
to incorporate knowledge of historical anomalies and remains
robust to unstable log data through semantic embedding. An
attention-based GRU neural network [20] is designed for

sequences, making it adaptable to more complex and dynamic
real-world situations.
B. LLM for log analysis
The comprehension abilities of large language models have
seen rapid improvement in recent years. There have been
numerous attempts to apply large language models to the
field of log analysis, including their application in log parsing
tasks [22]–[24]. Additionally, some efforts have been made to
utilize large language models for log anomaly detection. Log-
Prompt [25] leverages LLMs and advanced prompt strategies
to perform zero-shot log analysis tasks, thereby significantly
improving performance and interpretability without requiring
in-domain training data. LogGPT [26] utilizes ChatGPT to
detect anomalies in log data, demonstrating promising results
and good interpretability. It represents the first attempt to use
ChatGPT for log-based anomaly detection, providing insights
into the potential of prompt-based models in this domain.
ScaleAD [27] is an anomaly detection framework that utilizes
a Trie-based Detection Agent for efficiency and integrates
feedback from LLMs, such as ChatGPT, to improve accuracy
and adaptability in identifying system anomalies in log data.
At the same time, LLM has been applied to other areas of
data mining [28]–[31] and log analysis [32], showing a strong
ability to solve existing problems.
However, directly applying LLMs for zero-shot log analysis
tasks often leads to suboptimal results. This is because LLM is
pre-trained on extensive corpora, and when applied in specific
vertical domains, errors in judgment may occur due to the lack
of domain-specific pre-training knowledge. Therefore, we pro-
pose the Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) model [33].
The RAG model enhances LLMs by incorporating a retrieval
mechanism, enabling it to retrieve relevant information from
extensive knowledge bases and generate results tailored to spe-
cific tasks. This combined retrieval and generation approach
helps address the issue of lacking prior knowledge in zero-shot
tasks, improving the model’s adaptability and performance in
vertical domains. By leveraging RAG, we are able to better
tackle the challenges in log analysis tasks, enhancing the
accuracy and reliability of the framework.
III. DESIGN OF LOGRAG
A. Overview
The framework of LogRAG, shown in Fig. 2, consists of
four stages: Log Processing stage, Log Representation Stage,
Anomaly Detection Stage, and Post Processing Stage using
RAG. We first use the log parsing technique to get the contents
and templates from raw logs in the Log Processing Stage. Then
in the Log Representation Stage, contents and templates will
be encoded in different ways to get the feature vector. For
the Anomaly Detection Stage, we use the pre-trained BERT
to encode the Log Token, which is then used to train a Deep
One-Class Classification model. However, a one-class model
usually determines all the logs that do not appear in the train
stage as anomalies, resulting in a high false positive rate. To
solve this problem, we introduce RAG in the post-processing
stage. We will use the log template at this stage because it
preserves more semantics without incurring excessive costs.
For the logs identified as abnormal by the one-class model, we
will retrieve the similar logs in the historical logs, construct
a prompt, and further judge whether the log is abnormal by
combining the understanding ability of LLM.
B. Log Processing
Logs are semi-structured or unstructured texts, containing
various fields such as timestamp, severity level, and raw mes-
sage. The parameters contained in raw logs such as block id
and ip address may hinder further log analysis [34], thus
before implementing anomaly detection, we need to use log
parsing technique to convert the raw logs into structured data.
In this work, we adopt Drain [35] for log parsing and use two
parts of parsed logs: Log Templates, Log Tokens.
1) Log Templates: Log parsing plays crucial roles in large-
scale log processing. Through log parsing, we can identify
common structures and semantics in logs and abstract them
into template forms, thereby effectively compressing and sim-
plifying logs. This abstraction not only helps to reduce the
storage cost of logs but also optimizes subsequent log analysis
processes, thereby improving the learning efficiency of models
on key information. Log templates not only reduce redundant
information, allowing anomaly detection models to focus more
on critical Log Token for improving detection accuracy and
efficiency but also lower the costs of model training and
LLM invocation. Additionally, for natural language processing
models such as LLMs, simplified log formats improve their
efficiency and speed in processing input text. This prepro-
cessing stage lays a more reliable foundation for subsequent
log analysis and anomaly detection while optimizing system
performance and resource utilization.
2) Log Tokens: For LLM, it is convenient to obtain the
semantic information through log templates, but the log parser
usually has some parsing error cases, and these parsing errors
will greatly affect the performance of anomaly detection [15].
Therefore, in the anomaly detection stage, Log Tokens are

C. Log Representation
In the Log Representation phase, we need to extract mean-
ingful semantic representations from both Log Tokens and Log
Templates using pre-trained models. While some pre-trained
language models like BERT [37] have undergone meticulous
training on extensive text data, directly applying these models
for semantic extraction on specific domain data (in this paper,
logs) may not be adequate. Therefore, it is necessary to fine-
tune or pre-train these models on log data. Several works have
utilized large-scale log data to pre-train language models [16],
[38], [39]. Here, we directly utilize the pre-trained model from
BigLog [39] to encode Log Tokens.
To ensure a comprehensive understanding of log data, it
is crucial to embed log templates alongside encoding Log
Tokens. Aligning the embedding method with the LLM used
in subsequent processing ensures consistency in semantic
representation between Log Tokens and Log Templates. This
alignment not only deepens our understanding of queries,
contexts, and relationships within log templates but also
streamlines the entire log processing pipeline. Maintaining
consistency between the embedding method and the LLM
model used for post-processing facilitates smoother integration
and interpretation of log data, ultimately leading to more
accurate and insightful analyses. In our implementation, we
use OpenAI’s text-embedding-ada-002 model1 to achieve this
embedding.
D. Log Anomaly Detection
Existing unsupervised or semi-supervised log anomaly de-
tection methods attempt to model the sequences of normal
logs from the training data and then predict future log events to
determine if a log is anomalous. However, logs generated by a
system are unknown, and it is impractical to model all possible
sequence scenarios. Therefore, we adopt a novel approach:
modeling the normality of normal logs, without the need for
complex log sequence information.
Motivated by Huo et al. [16], we utilize DeepSVDD [40] as
the backbone for learning the normality of normal logs in the
anomaly detection model training. DeepSVDD is particularly
suited for this task because it simplifies the problem of
anomaly detection to a one-class classification model, where
the main objective is to enclose the data points from the
’normal’ class within a minimal hypersphere in the feature
space. This method offers a robust way to capture the compact
and common features of normal logs, as it pushes anomalies,
which do not conform to the normal log pattern, outside the
hypersphere.
In the specific context of our task, our objective is to map
the features of normal log templates to the interior of a hyper-
sphere using a neural network. Normal log templates should
ideally be close to the center of the hypersphere. To achieve

E. Post-processing Using RAG
After the anomaly detection stage, we can identify a
significant number of abnormal logs. However, due to the
characteristics of the single-class model, this stage tends to
classify normal logs that have not appeared in historical data
as anomalies directly, leading to a high false positive rate. To
address this issue, the logs identified by the anomaly detection
stage as anomalies need to undergo further examination. In our
implementation, we first store the vector representation of the
Log Templates obtained through the Log Representation Stage
in a vector database, then we employ Retrieval-Augmented
Generation (RAG) to perform a secondary assessment of the
logs identified as anomalies in the anomaly detection stage.
Specifically, for each anomalous log detected in the anomaly
detection stage, we obtain its log template. Subsequently, we
retrieve the log with the highest similarity to the template from
the vector database. Following that, we construct a prompt
based on the logs to be evaluated and the retrieval results,
leveraging the comprehension capabilities of large language
models, to make a final determination on whether the log is
anomalous.
1) Retrieve Strategies: In our implementation, efficient
retrieval of log templates similar to the log under consideration
from the vector database is crucial. To achieve this, we
employ cosine distance as our metric to calculate the similarity
between two vectors, vi and vj as depicted in Eq.(3). Cosine
distance is chosen due to its effectiveness in high-dimensional
spaces commonly found in text-related tasks, allowing for
a measurement that is more robust to variations in vector
magnitude:
Similarity(vi, vj ) = vi · vj ∥vi∥ · ∥vj ∥ (3)
To ensure the relevance and precision of our retrieval, we
constrain the process by setting a similarity threshold and spec-
ifying the desired number of retrieval results. Specifically, we
only consider templates for which the cosine similarity score
exceeds 0.8. This threshold was determined based on empirical
tests that showed it effectively balances between precision and
recall, optimizing for the most relevant results without overly
narrowing the search scope. As a result, we designate the top 5

2) Prompt Construction: As described in Logprompt [25],
when it comes to handling log analysis tasks, the chain of
thought (CoT) [41] method can standardize the intermediate
reasoning process in LLM, leading to more accurate judgment
results. Therefore, we carefully designed prompts in which we
outline the specific judgment process. This process implicitly
creates a rational chain of reasoning, resulting in more logi-
cally sound decisions [25].
As shown in Fig. 3, the constructed prompt consists of
four parts: Task Description, Input Templates, CoT Judgement
Process, and Output Format. In the Task Description, we
explicitly outline the tasks that LLM needs to accomplish.
In addition to determining normal/abnormal, we require that
it provides reasons for its judgment results. On one hand, by
having LLM offer reasonable explanations for its judgments,
we can clarify its analytical process and facilitate further
debugging. On the other hand, this approach enables us to
provide users with detailed analysis reports, offering a clearer
understanding of the system’s operational status.
We need to apply the same prompt for the detection of
each log template. To this end, we have designated two
variable parts: {Input Log Template} and {Topk Known
Normal Log Templates}, within the Input Templates. These
parts are intended to represent respectively the log template
under evaluation and the retrieved similar log template. To de-
termine the abnormality of an input log template, the three-step
process is employed in the CoT Judgement Process. Firstly,
the template is compared with known normal log templates for
structural and semantic similarities. If the template is identified
as normal, the classification concludes. If not, the analysis
proceeds to the next step. In the third step, the input log
template is independently examined, focusing on keywords
and key elements such as error codes. It is crucial to note
that parameters represented by <*> should not be considered
as indicative of abnormality. The wildcard symbol in the log
template is used to represent variable parts, and for this task,
the input specifically consists of these log templates. Finally,
we enforce strict control over LLM’s output, mandating that
it produces a response in JSON format. This response should
include information on whether the log template is deemed
abnormal and provide the corresponding reasons.
3) LLM: When providing a specific prompt to LLM, we
populate the <Input Templates> with concrete log templates.
The log template under consideration is filled in as {Input
Log Template}, whereas the retrieved similar log templates
are filled into {Topk Known Normal Log Templates}.
In our primary experiments, we utilized OpenAI’s ChatGPT
as the specific implementation of the LLM. However, with the
rapid development of open-source LLMs, some of them have
surpassed ChatGPT in certain capabilities. We are also curious
about evaluating the performance of open-source LLMs in
log anomaly detection tasks. Considering the privacy and
cost concerns associated with real-world application data, the
utilization of open-source LLMs is a promising alternative.
Accordingly, we conducted tests using both ChatGPT and an
open-source LLM.
Another crucial point is that responses from LLM exhibit
diversity and occasionally do not strictly adhere to the spec-
ified format in the prompt [26]. Therefore, it is necessary to
evaluate the response results. In cases where the format does
not meet the requirements, we use ChatGPT to further parse
the unexpected text until it conforms to our processing needs.
IV. EXPERIMENTS
In this section, we will evaluate LogRAG by answing the
following research questions.
• RQ1: How does LogRAG perform in anomaly detection
compared with the state-of-the-art methods?
• RQ2: How RAG contributes to LogRAG in online
anomaly detection?
• RQ3: How do different prompt statements affect the
performance of LogRAG?
• RQ4: How does each component of LogRAG contribute
to the final anomaly detection result?
A. Experimental Setup
1) Datasets: We conduct our experiments on two widely-
used open datasets (BGL, Spirit) from LogHub [42]. Each log
in these two datasets was manually labeled as abnormal and
normal. BGL dataset is a supercomputing system log dataset
collected by Lawrence Livermore National Labs (LLNL) [43].
The BGL dataset contains 4,747,963 logs, 348,460 of which
are labeled as abnormal (7.34%). Spirit dataset is an aggrega-
tion of system log data from the Spirit supercomputing system
at Sandia National Labs [43]. In this experiment, we only
use the first 5 million log lines of the original dataset, which
contains 764,500 abnormal log lines (15.29%).
2) Evaluation Metrics: Our approach does not require
complex log sequence information during the training process.
However, some methods (e.g., LogAnomaly [12] and PLELog
[13]) are based on log sequences for anomaly detection.
Therefore, for the sake of fairness, we uniformly adopt
sequence-based standards for evaluation during the testing
phase. Specifically, we obtain log sequences with a window
size of 200 logs and 0.5 hours at regular time intervals. If there
is at least one log entry in the sequence that is anomalous, the
sequence is considered anomalous; otherwise, it is considered
normal. It is important to emphasize that some recent works
shuffle the dataset before subsequent training. However, this
approach leads to the model seeing log data from the future
during the training phase (i.e., data leakage), resulting in
inflated prediction performance [44]. We adhere to real-world
scenarios and do not shuffle the data; instead, we partition
the data in chronological order. We select the first 80% of
each dataset based on their timestamps for training and use
the remaining 20% for testing.

3) Baselines: We compare LogRAG with the following
state-of-the-art log anomaly detection methods.
• DeepLog [1] is the first work to utilize deep learning
methods for log anomaly detection. It models normal
system executions using deep neural networks and can
continuously update over time.
• LogAnomaly [12] models log streams as natural lan-
guage sequences, using a novel method called tem-
plate2vec to extract semantic information from log tem-
plates and detect both sequential and quantitative anoma-
lies.
• PLELog [13] is a semi-supervised log-based anomaly
detection approach that incorporates knowledge of his-
torical anomalies through probabilistic label estimation,
uses semantic embedding to handle unstable log data,
and employs an attention-based GRU neural network for
efficient and effective anomaly detection.
• LogRobust [14] is a supervised learning method that
represents log events as semantic vectors. It then employs
attention-based Bi-LSTM to capture contextual informa-
tion from log sequences, enabling anomaly detection in
unstable log events and sequences.
4) Implementation Details and Environments: In our exper-
iments, we used the Adam optimizer with an initial learning
rate of 0.0001, training the model for 150 epochs. The learning
rate was adjusted at epoch 50 using a milestone schedule.
A batch size of 40,960 was chosen, and a weight decay of
0.0005 was applied to regularize the model. We conducted
all the experiments on the same Linux server with a 64
Intel(R) Xeon(R) Gold 6226R CPU @ 2.90GHz CPU, 256GB
RAM, and a GPU with 24GB memory. The operating system
is Ubuntu 20.04.1. We implemented LogRAG using Python
3.8.18 and PyTorch 1.10.1. For the post-processing stage
utilizing the Language Models in RAG, we employed both
OpenAI’s API interface using ChatGPT-3.5-turbo and the
Mistral-7B-Instruct model provided by Hugging Face2. For
other anomaly detection methods, we utilized their publicly
available implementations [44]. Our code and experiments are
available on our project homepage3.

V. CASE STUDY
As previously discussed, employing RAG for post-
processing results from deep one-class classifiers can sig-
nificantly enhance the precision of log anomaly detection.
In this section, we specifically explore the errors that RAG
corrects in the detection process of deep one-class classifiers.
Based on the comparison of detection results before and after
applying RAG, the false positive templates corrected by RAG
are divided into the following three major types as shown in
Table IV.
Type 1: The log template matches the templates in the
training logs. To mitigate the impact of log parsing errors
on log anomaly detection, we employed Log Tokens instead
of Log Templates when training the deep one-class classifier,
thereby reducing the influence of log parsing errors while
also increasing false positives. For instance, some templates
corresponding to logs identified as anomalies were already
present in the training log templates. Therefore, during the
RAG post-processing stage, retrieving similar templates from
the vector database enabled the recovery of these previously
encountered log templates. Moreover, based on the prompts
we constructed, the LLM can readily determine that these log
templates are normal. As shown in Table IV, for Type 1, the
two logs under review retrieved identical log templates from
the historical logs, which were confirmed as normal. Using
the prompts, the LLM classified this log template as normal,
thus reducing the false positives in this category.
Type 2: The log template is both structurally and
semantically identical or similar to the templates in the
training logs. System updates may alter the original logs,
yielding new logs that are not identical to historical logs;
however, the structure and semantics of these new logs remain
consistent with those of the historical logs. For the false
positives arising in such instances, our specially designed RAG
module can effectively recognize them. Initially, through the
retrieval module, it is possible to find logs similar to the current
log. For instance, as shown in Table IV under Type 2, the
new log total of <*> ddr error(s) detected and corrected
over <*> seconds when compared to the retrieved similar log
total of <*> ddr error(s) detected and corrected merely adds
temporal information; nonetheless, the structure and semantics
remain unchanged. Consequently, it can be classified as a
normal log, thereby preventing the misclassification of this
type of log as an anomaly.
Type 3: The log template failed to retrieve any similar
templates from the training logs and lacks explicit anomaly
information. Additionally, some logs generated after system
updates do not match any similar logs in historical data. Based
on the prompts we constructed, the LLM must independently
determine whether the log is an anomaly, considering both the
keywords and the overall semantic information in the template.
For those templates that lack explicit anomaly keywords or
where the overall semantics do not indicate an anomaly,
the RAG module can effectively recognize these as normal.
For instance, as shown in Table IV under Type 3, the log
lmgrd shutdown succeeded includes the keyword shutdown,
potentially suggesting an anomaly; however, its overall se-
mantics signify the successful completion of an event, thereby
classifying it as normal. Following RAG post-processing, this
method effectively prevents the misclassification of such logs
as anomalies.
The three types of logs described are continuously gen-
erated during all system operations and updates. During its
post-processing stage, LogRAG can effectively identify the
aforementioned three types of logs misclassified as anoma-
lies, thereby significantly reducing the false positive rate and
enhancing the system’s overall usability.

VII. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
This paper presents LogRAG, a semi-supervised framework
for log anomaly detection. It incorporates a deep single-class
classifier for anomaly detection and employs RAG for post-
processing. In contrast to other semi-supervised log detection
methods, LogRAG alleviates the effects of log parsing errors
and unstable log sequences resulting from system evolution.
Moreover, through the utilization of RAG, LogRAG dimin-
ishes false positives in the final outcomes. The experimental re-
sults suggest that LogRAG surpasses all other semi-supervised
anomaly detection methods in terms of F1 Score, approaching
the performance of supervised methods.
For future research, we plan to evaluate the performance of
various LLMs within the LogRAG framework and develop
more effective retrieval strategies and prompts to further
enhance the framework’s effectiveness. We also aim to in-
tegrate LogRAG with other advanced log anomaly detection
methods to enhance their overall effectiveness. Additionally,
we will explore more effective approaches for log anomaly
detection using LLMs, including the development of improved
model architectures and feature selection strategies tailored to
different detection scenarios.
